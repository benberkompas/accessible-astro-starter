## Context

### Team alignment isn’t a solved problem

I’ve often seen teams struggle because each person carries a slightly different mental model of how the system should work. Those differences usually come to the surface late, when the cost of reconciling them is high, and create tension (or downright conflict) at the very moment when the team should be gaining momentum.

Generating more output is easier than it’s ever been. Anyone can crank out ideas, UI concepts, prototypes, or even rubrics for evaluating and grading the quality of these things. However, none of this addresses the root cause behind team misalignment, which is rarely a lack of ideas.

More often, teams have an abundance of ideas but struggle to connect them together over time in a way that results in a strong *system*. There can be too much <a href="/portfolio/aligning-conceptual-models-for-relational-accountability" target="_blank">presumed alignment</a> and not enough *actual* alignment.

## Frame

### What are users interfacing with?

There are many different interfaces involved in software development. There are Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) which allow separate systems to exchange information; one makes a request, the other returns a response. Then, of course, there are User Interfaces (UI) which is a term we often use interchangeably with screen design. We once distinguished between Graphical User Interfaces (GUIs) and other types of UI, but now every possible meaning for the phrase is lumped together under "UI", and some go even further, treating UI and UX as interchangeable...

This suggests we have lost sight of what the user interface is actually for.

The UI has two jobs:

1. It must faithfully express the underlying system
2. And provide a way for users to interface with it in order to get things done

*How* they do this - whether tapping a screen, dictating commands, having an agent act on their behalf, etc. - depends entirely on what the interface allows and the capabilities of the underlying system.

<FramedImage
  src="/projects/orca-navigator/user-interface-system.svg"
  alt="Placeholder"

/>

Either way, they're interfacing with the system, so it needs to be designed carefully. 

### Object-Oriented UX

Designers play a crucial role in system design and that's where I start every project.

I've found the best way to approach this is with Object-Oriented UX, which is first a philosophy of design that recognizes humans are wired to percieve the world -- including the digital world -- in terms of objects.

It also provides a flexible methodology. We begin by identifying the key objects -- the nouns, the things that matter to people, and which should provide the conceptual bedrock of the system. From there, we ask how those objects relate to each other, including questions of cardinality (i.e. how many of one thing relate to how many of another thing). This gives us a sturdy *network* of connected concepts. Next we identify what actions may be taken on each object and who make take those actions; this is where <a href="/portfolio/roles-and-permissions" target="_blank">roles and permissions</a> enter the scene, and, yes, they get a lot of our attention! Finally, we look at the attributes of each object, the things that make it meaningful, recognizable, and useful to people.

* **O**bjects
* **R**elationships
* **C**alls to action
* **A**ttributes

It's simple, but elegant, and has helped me enormously when I work on really complex systems. I have a "hook" for virtually every question or problem that comes up.

## Crystallize

Here are a few examples of how this has played out in my work.

### A cardinality conflict

We were exploring subscription sharing. Three objects were involved: subscription, user, and account.

<FramedImage
  src="/projects/orca-navigator/account-cardinality.png"
  alt="Placeholder"
  caption="Each account could have one-to-many users."
/>

Long-term we wanted each account to have *only one user*, but this would mean people could no longer share a subscription, which was a particular concern for families.

The key here was cardinality: *How many of one thing relate to how many of another thing?*

Should we pivot from our <a href="/portfolio/account-structure" target="_blank">historic account structure</a> and enforce a "one user per account" rule? Would that alienate multi-user accounts? Might some of them cancel their subscription? Would it be worth it?

No amount of UI exploration would have revealed the answer, because this is fundamentally a system design question.

The team gained alignment in a matter of days, and the project was set aside because it conflicted with our long-term strategy and didn't offer sufficient upside to be worth the cost and risk. I look back on this decision and am *very* thankful we didn't pursue it further!

### Device as a first-class object

I'm currently exploring an opportunity involving these three objects: device, activity, and user.

In our system today, device isn't really an object —- it's an *attribute* of activity that tags along for the ride. When activity is sent to the server, device often accompanies it.

This can cause some very unexpected behavior for users, who want to know that all devices are accounted for and running properly.

A device's *status* and even *existence* within the system depends on there being recent activity. Sometimes there's a lapse in activity -- not because anything is wrong, but simply because of a user setting, or a device not being used -- but this still causes the device to appear "inactive", which is alarming to some of our users.

<FramedImage
  src="/projects/orca-navigator/device-as-first-class.png"
  alt="Placeholder"
  caption="I've been proposing that we treat device as a first-class object, and have spent significant time modeling what that would mean."
/>

The implications are substantial.

* We could build a much more reliable way of communicating device status and stop relying on recent activity as our only signal
* We could finally address a deeper account structure challenge related to family accounts where young children are treated as authenticated users, required to have an email address, and parents must reuse their email address for their children; this results in duplicate accounts because we can't flag email addresses as "already taken"
* We could offer device-level options for our filtering service, which would reduce the need for people to use hacky workarounds when they need to set up their personal and work devices differently

I've presented these concepts to our member care team and engineering leadership, and some are gaining traction largely because I’m framing them as system-level structural changes, not simply UI improvements.

## Sustain

### I built the tool I needed

Whiteboards are great for thinking together, but I needed a way to capture and encode those decisions in a more structured format.

I turned to JSON, which is great for the machines but not so easy for humans.

So I challenged myself to build a custom tool that treats JSON as a lightweight backend and gives me practical ways to view the model, evaluate decisions, and maintain a living reference of the system.

<FramedImage
  src="/projects/orca-navigator/json-viewer.png"
  alt="Placeholder"
  caption="I chose to use JSON as a lightweight backend because it's very easy to write, simple to customize, and wouldn't require me to learn how to build and manage a database."
/>

### From shared tool to background engine

When I first built this tool, I envisioned putting it in everyone's hands. I ran into obstacles making that happen across the organization, so instead, I adapted; the tool became a background engine for my own practice. 

I'm currently building Claude skills so others can extract the things they need without wading too deep into the theory.
